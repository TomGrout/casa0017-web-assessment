River Lea, east London

Where does the sewage go after it rains?

When it rains hard, London's sewers overflow into the River Lea. You would expect the river to carry the sewage out to the Thames. But the bottom end of the Lea is tidal, and when the tide is coming in it can push the sewage back up toward Stratford.

We built this site to show when that happens.

Step 1 Rain Walthamstow

London's sewers carry rain and sewage in the same pipe.

Much of London's sewer network was built in the 1860s and 1870s, and it was designed to take rainwater from roofs and roads in the same pipes as waste from homes. On a dry day all of it flows to the treatment works.

Heavy rain fills those pipes. So that sewage doesn't back up into streets and houses, the extra is let out through storm overflows: pipes, called outfalls, that empty straight into the river.

Step 2 Spill River Lea

Some outfalls spill after very little rain.

Not every spill needs a storm. If the ground is already wet, or the pipes are already full, a light shower can be enough to tip an outfall over.

For every spill we record, we add up the rain that fell at Walthamstow in the 24 hours before it started. Put side by side, those totals show which outfalls are the first to go.

Step 3 Tide Silvertown

Then the tide decides where it goes.

The bottom end of the Lea, Bow Creek, is tidal. Twice a day the Thames rises and pushes water up the creek, and twice a day it falls and draws it back out.

Sewage that spills on a falling tide is carried out to the Thames. Sewage that spills on a rising tide can be pushed back upstream, into the channels and canal basins around Stratford. A map of spills can't show this, so this is what we set out to measure.
